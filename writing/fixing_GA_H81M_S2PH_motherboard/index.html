Fixing a GA-H81M-S2PH motherboard

Written by: Matthew

Originally posted: 23rd of June, 2026
Last updated: 23rd of June, 2026

Oh no, my computer is not working!

Around late 2024, my dad was using his desktop computer as normal until one day it stopped working. I opened up the case and my troubleshooting led me to find out that some small SMD component was fried near the 24 pin ATX power connector on the motherboard, thus rendering the machine inoperable. What I think happened was that the generic power supply in the machine went rogue and pumped bad voltages into the motherboard. I guess you really shouldn't cheap out on power supplies after all.

I looked online and I found that a replacement motherboard would cost a ridiculous 50-ish dollars. Instead of that, we decided to buy new parts. They would be more reliable anyways, as those parts were heading on ten years old at that point. The new parts ended up being an A520M motherboard, Ryzen 5 processor and 16gb of DDR4 ram [1]. For reference, the old parts were on a LGA 1150 platform which included a Pentium G3220 and 4gb of DDR3 ram. I reassembled everything, and everything thankfully worked [2]. However, I still had this fried GIGABYTE GA-H81M-S2PH motherboard lying around. So the question was: Could I potentially fix it?

First attempts

The first problem in my attempt to fix the motherboard was the fact that I didn't have access to a multimeter. However, I knew that my school had some. So the plan was for me to take the motherboard along with a power supply to school, and then work on the problem during my self-studying periods in the electronics lab (with permission from the teacher Mr. R).

The symptoms which the motherboard exhibited when I first began working in it was that when power was applied to the board, the fans continuously cycled on and off every five seconds or so. There was no video output at all when connected to a monitor.

I first started out by drawing a pinout for the 24 pin ATX power. I specifically wanted to find out what that blown up passive component was near the 24 pin ATX power connector. While I was unable to find a schematic diagram for my exact board model number, I did find one for the GA-H81M-S2PT [3], which was very similar to my board. I did eventually find out what pins connected to that blown up component using the continuity function on the multimeter. At this point, I can't remember what exact voltage rail it was, but I am pretty sure it was a voltage rail anyways.

I was getting my hopes up at this point in time! If I somehow managed to replace that component then I could potentially have a working motherboard again.

Just to get a second opinion however, I explained what I had found so far to Mr. R. He gave me the bad news though. The 24 pin ATX connector is designed to have many pins to power the same voltage rails of 12v, 5v and 3.3v. This meant that even if one of the pins were not connected properly, then the other pins would still apply an adequate amount of power to the board. Essentially, this blown up component could not have been the issue.

Being the cool guy he is though, he suggested that I should use his thermal camera for troubleshooting. The idea is that any shorted/faulty components would heat up much quicker than any of the other components, which would show up on the camera's display. A few days later, I walked into the electronics lab to find a thermal camera sitting on the teacher's desk.

Thermal camera time!

I first had to charge the thermal camera for a few minutes as the battery was completely dead. In the box, there were also some lenses which you can attach to the front camera. These lenses "magnify" the camera so you can get much closer to an object without losing focus. This was exactly what I needed in my scenario, as I had to point the thermal camera pretty close to the PCB.

I applied power to the board and started looking around. I would love to have found a shorted resistor at 80 degrees celsius or something but it wasn't that easy. I couldn't find anything getting abnormally hot. The only thing that even looked slightly suspicious was a couple resistors near the platform controller hub. I removed them from the board completely and tested the board again. Surprise surprise, this did nothing to fix the board. Unfortunately, this was another dead end in my journey to fix this board.

Checking for shorts

I switched the multimeter into continuity mode again and started probing around any capacitor for shorts. The idea was that you can tell if a capacitor is shorted or not by placing your probes on the positive and negative ends. If the multimeter beeps, then you know that the capacitor is shorted. After doing this for a while, I found out that pretty much all of the capacitors which I checked were showing up as shorted. That seemed strange, how could so many capacitors be shorted?

Confused by the situation, I asked Mr. R and he explained what could potentially be happening. He also drew up this quick diagram pictured below.

If I remember correctly, he explained that if one of the capacitors/components in a parallel circuit becomes shorted, this can make other components also show up as shorted even if they are not. The diagram above shows us that if the bottom capacitor is fouled, then if you try to check if the top capacitor then it will also show up as shorted.

After a bit more probing around, I soon concluded that the bad power supply fried the PCH chip, causing it to short internally. Since there was no way that I was learning BGA rework, and also the fact that I didn't have access to any hot air station, I made the executive decision to just give up on the project.

Spoiler: I failed

Yes, in the end I failed to fix the motherboard. Though strange enough, I don't think this journey was as big of a failure as you might at first think. This is because I learnt a few things along the way.

For starters, I learnt how to use a multimeter. Up until that point in time I had watched a few videos on how to use a multimeter though I never used one in practice. Now, I definitely feel like I have a much better grasp on what the different features are and how to use them. I can confidently measure DC voltages for example.

I also got to mess around with a thermal camera. I mean, these things are not cheap and I find them fun to play around with. Even by itself, this experience would have been worth it just for that.

I think this just goes to show how even if you fail at achieving your primary objective (whatever it might be), you can still consider something a success because of all the things you have learnt during that journey. We idolize the idea of being "perfect", yet we often miss out on the fact that we often have to fail a lot in order to learn and gain important experience. You can't be truly perfect, yet you can always learn new things which can make you ever so closer to the popular objective of living a perfect life.
