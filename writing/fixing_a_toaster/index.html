Fixing a toaster

Written by: Matthew

Originally posted: 24th of June, 2026
Last updated: 24th of June, 2026

Problem

A year or so ago, our decade old Breville Freestyle toaster started acting up. No matter how you set the browning dial, the toast either came out too raw or too burnt. Goldilocks would not be happy. Anyways, to solve this problem, my family bought the cheapest toaster that we could find at the time and moved on.

But I didn't move on. Before we threw it out, of course I had to figure out if I could fix it.

My theory as to what was happening was that the potentiometer that was controlling the browning dial had gone faulty. This meant that the resistance values that it was giving the microcontroller was making the toaster act all funny. Therefore, the plan was to take it all apart so that I could get to the circuit board, and to test the potentiometer with a multimeter. This would confirm whether or not it was at fault.

Troubleshooting

The toaster was quite easy to take apart, minus the decades worth of breadcrumbs which constantly fell out during disassembly. There are a few philips head screws underneath, with some hiding behind the removable breadcrumb tray as well. I also had to remove the plastic browning dial and the plastic push down handle. Soon enough, I had the circuit board taken out.

At the time, I didn't have access to a multimeter at home so instead, I took the circuit board to the electronics lab at my school. I also brought along some desoldering braid that I had at home, so I could use their soldering iron to remove the potentiometer from the board. Unfortunately, you cannot measure resistance reliably while a component is still in circuit, so this step was necessary.

It took an embarrassingly long time, maybe half an hour to actually desolder it. I tried to use those handheld solder suckers at first, but they didn't do anything. My desoldering braid saved me that day.

Sure enough, when I checked with the multimeter the potentiometer turned out bad. The resistance values were jumping all over the place. When I asked the teacher Mr. R what was happening, he explained that this is what is known as "bouncing". This was most likely due to internal wear inside of the potentiometer, which would necessitate a full replacement if I wanted to fix the issue.

The MP4 video below shows what a good 10k ohm potentiometer looks like. The readings are very smooth as I go through its range.

However, this MP4 video below shows what readings I got from the potentiometer from the toaster. As you can see, the values jump all over the place as I turn the knob.

The bad news from all of this is that the potentiometer was rated at a weird 250k ohms resistance. This was much higher than anything Mr. R could find laying around in the electronics lab. Furthermore, the potentiometers that he could find were not in the right form factor. This is important, as the plastic knob needs to fit securely on the potentiometer in order for it to work. Unfortunately, if I wanted this thing fixed then I would be alone from this point onwards.

Research (and disappointment)

I started looking online in places like Mouser and Digikey. At first, I thought I was straight out of luck. I couldn't find any which looked anything like the one I needed. I persisted through my searches though, and I soon found what I was looking for. Introducing the BOURNS PDB12-H4201-254BF (Datasheet), the exact replacement part that I needed. Even better, it was in stock!

Cool right? All I needed to do was to order and get it shipped to my door and...

Yeah, there is no way I am spending $28.60 on a single potentiometer. I mean, you can buy another supermarket toaster for that price!

At this point, I decided to end my journey here. The toaster shall never be fixed after all.
